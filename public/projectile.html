Projectile motion, from the ground up

A projectile is an object that has been launched and then moves only under gravity. This lab uses the AP Physics 1 model: a point mass, no air resistance, constant g downward, and a landing height equal to the launch height.

1. Two independent motions

The most important idea is that the curved path is not a special new kind of motion. It is two ordinary one-dimensional motions happening at the same time.

Horizontal

No horizontal force, so no horizontal acceleration.

ax = 0
vx = constant = v0 cos θ
x(t) = vx t

Vertical

Gravity is the only vertical acceleration. Up is positive here.

ay = −g
vy(t) = v0 sin θ − g t
y(t) = v0 sin θ · t − ½ g t²

Because ax = 0, the x velocity never changes. Because ay = −g, the y velocity steadily decreases, is zero at the top, and is equally large but downward when the projectile lands.

2. Launch angle splits the initial speed

The launcher does not choose vx and vy separately. It chooses one speed v0 and an angle θ above the horizontal.

vx = v0 cos θ
vy = v0 sin θ

  • A small angle puts almost all of v0 into vx. The projectile is fast horizontally but barely leaves the ground.
  • A steep angle puts almost all of v0 into vy. The projectile hangs in the air longer and goes higher, but crawls horizontally.
  • At 45°, sin θ = cos θ, so the two components start equal.

3. Time of flight

Landing happens when y returns to zero (after launch). Set y(t) = 0:

0 = (v0 sin θ) t − ½ g t²
t (v0 sin θ − ½ g t) = 0

One root is t = 0 (launch). The other is the landing time:

tflight = 2 v0 sin θ / g

Flight time depends only on the vertical part of the launch. Larger θ or larger v0 means more hang time. Larger g brings the projectile down sooner.

4. Maximum height — where vy = 0

The apex is not “where the path looks highest.” It is the instant vertical velocity is zero. After that, gravity has finished canceling the upward component and the projectile starts falling.

0 = v0 sin θ − g tapex
tapex = v0 sin θ / g
H = v0² sin²θ / (2g)

Notice tapex is exactly half the flight time when launch and landing heights are equal. Height keeps increasing as θ approaches 90°. A vertical launch has the greatest height and zero range.

5. Horizontal range

Range is how far the projectile travels horizontally during the flight: R = vx tflight. Substitute the two pieces:

R = (v0 cos θ)(2 v0 sin θ / g)
R = v0² (2 sin θ cos θ) / g
R = v0² sin(2θ) / g

The identity sin(2θ) = 2 sin θ cos θ is why complementary angles matter. sin(2θ) has a maximum of 1 when 2θ = 90°, so θ = 45° gives the greatest range when the landing height equals the launch height.

Rmax = v0² / g    at    θ = 45°

6. Why 30° and 60° land equally far

sin(2θ) is symmetric about 45°. For any angle θ, the complement 90° − θ has the same sin(2θ) and therefore the same range.

  • 30° and 60°: same range, but 60° goes higher and stays up longer.
  • 20° and 70°, 35° and 55°, and so on: same pattern.
  • Greater height does not mean greater range. 60° beats 30° in height and loses to 45° in range.

7. Worked example

Use the lab defaults: v0 = 20 m/s, g = 9.8 m/s², θ = 45°.

vx = 20 cos 45° = 14.1 m/s
vy = 20 sin 45° = 14.1 m/s
tflight = 2(20)sin 45° / 9.8 = 2.89 s
H = (20)² sin²45° / (2 × 9.8) = 10.2 m
R = (20)² sin 90° / 9.8 = 40.8 m

In the lab, the numerical stepper uses dt = 0.01 s, so the displayed landing values should be within a few tenths of a meter of these closed-form results. Teacher view compares the two.

9. What this model leaves out

  • Air resistance would shorten range, lower the best angle below 45°, and break the 30°/60° symmetry.
  • If the landing is below the launch point, the best angle is less than 45°.
  • If the landing is above the launch point, the best angle is greater than 45°.

Those extensions are later physics. This lab is built so you can see the equal-height case cleanly.
